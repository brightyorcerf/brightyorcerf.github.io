Squeezing YOLOv8n into orbit

I wanted to know how much of a satellite's imagery needs to come down at all, if the satellite can work out what's in a frame before it transmits anything. To test it, I set myself a constraint: run object detection on multispectral imagery with 2 CPU cores and 4GB of RAM, roughly the compute of a small satellite's onboard computer, and stay under 400ms per frame at p99. No GPU.

Stock YOLOv8n at FP32 came in at around 560ms p99 on that profile, over budget before any variance under load.

Why I didn't drop bands

YOLOv8n is already the smallest model in the YOLOv8 family, so a smaller architecture wasn't really on the table. The other obvious way to cut compute was to feed it fewer channels. The input is six-band multispectral, not RGB, and dropping bands cost more accuracy than anything else I tried. That left quantization: INT8 instead of FP32, through ONNX Runtime.

Paying for INT8

INT8 gives up accuracy to win back latency and memory, and how much it gives up depends on your model and your data. Published quantization benchmarks told me little about mine. I used static post-training quantization in ONNX Runtime, calibrated on a few hundred frames from the target domain rather than a generic dataset. If you calibrate on the wrong distribution, accuracy can drop quietly, and you won't notice until you've committed to the quantized weights.

The final layers of the detection head stayed out of the INT8 pass. Quantizing them cost a lot of accuracy on small objects and saved little latency, so that was the one place I kept precision on purpose.

Quantization alone closed the gap. I expected to need operator fusion or batching on top of it, and I didn't.

Deciding what to send down

A detection made in orbit is useless if the downlink can't carry it, and downlink is scarcer than compute. So the preprocessor also decides what's worth transmitting. Detections are serialized into protobuf briefs. SGP4, the standard model for predicting a satellite's position from its published orbital elements, tells the system in advance which pass windows matter, so it doesn't spend compute or bandwidth outside them.

Sending briefs instead of raw frames cut the payload by 1,484×. That ratio depends on how many detections a frame holds, because what comes down is a list of findings, not the image.

Next

Mixed precision in the first convolution, the only layer where the six bands are still separate. Some bands probably carry more detection signal than others, and giving them all the same INT8 treatment may be costing accuracy or latency I haven't measured.
